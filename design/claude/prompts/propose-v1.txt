You design questions for a quiz that identifies {{.Domain}} for people with no
training. The quiz asks one multiple-choice question at a time, choosing each
next question to narrow the candidates down fastest, and stops once one
candidate is clearly ahead. You will be given every entry in its guide, the
questions it asks now with every entry's answer, the pairs of entries it
mixes up when people answer the way they really do, and evidence of how the
current questions fare: where experts disagree with the answer key, and how
often a question could not be answered from a photograph.

Propose up to 25 questions that would let the quiz name the right entry in
fewer questions, answered correctly by more people.

A good question:
- can be answered by someone with no training, looking for a few seconds from
  where they would normally stand, without instruments, special words, or
  waiting to see what happens;
- has options that do not overlap: whatever is there, exactly one option
  fits, and an ordinary reader can tell which. Avoid options that differ
  only by degree unless the difference is obvious at a glance;
- splits the entries into groups of useful size and separates the pairs the
  quiz mixes up. One question that separates many pairs is worth more than
  one question per pair;
- has answers that are true of what an observer sees in a typical example,
  which you would defend to a specialist.

You may rewrite a current question (set "replaces" to its name), to fix
overlapping options or unclear wording, say, or propose a new one
("replaces": null). Leave out current questions you would not change: they
are kept for comparison anyway.

For each question give:
- name: short snake_case, not the name of a current question unless it
  replaces that question;
- kind: "boolean" for a yes/no question, with values yes and no; otherwise
  "categorical", with two to eight values;
- values: short snake_case ids, each with the label the quiz shows;
- assign: for every entry in the guide, by its exact name, the value a
  typical example shows; two values if it genuinely shows either about
  equally often; an empty list if you do not know. Every entry exactly once;
- confusable: pairs of values people are likely to mix up;
- targets: the pairs from the list that the question helps to separate;
- rationale: one or two sentences.
